Rope, and the order in which it fails

The yard opens before the light does. Whoever arrives first turns the compressor on and leaves the doors wide, because the smell of last night's varnish will otherwise sit in the rafters until noon and make everybody short tempered by the middle of the morning. There is a rhythm to it that nobody writes down and everybody follows.

A rope does not part all at once. It gives up a few fibres at a time, in the place where it turns, and for a long while the only sign is a slight softness under the thumb that a person who has handled a great deal of rope will notice and a person who has not will walk straight past. The failure is therefore never a surprise to the yard and always a surprise to the owner.

This is the argument for looking at a thing rather than being told about it. An instrument that reports what it measured is useful. An instrument that reports what it did not measure is more useful still, and rarer, because saying so costs it the appearance of competence.

The same is true of a tide table, which is a prediction and is printed as though it were an observation. Nobody who works on the water confuses the two, and everybody who reads the table from an office does.

By this point in a long page a reader is scrolling rather than reading, and a reviewer is clicking rather than scrolling. The gap between those two states is where everything expensive happens. A screenshot flattens a page into one artifact, and the artifact carries all of it — the part that was read, the part that was skimmed, and the part below the fold that was never on screen at all.

Varnish is applied in coats so thin that the first three look like a mistake. The fourth is the first one that shows, and by the eighth the grain is under glass and the whole thing looks inevitable. It is not inevitable; it is eight coats. The visible result and the work that produced it have almost nothing in common, and one of them is much easier to photograph.

After the last mark, which is where a mark-to-mark walk stops

A tour of the outlined blocks ends at the last outlined block. Whatever is written after it is, as far as that tour is concerned, not part of the picture. On this page that is another eight hundred vertical pixels of ordinary text, and on a real one it is frequently the footer with the account summary in it.

The winter cover is cut oversize and taken in twice, because a cover that fits in October does not fit in February when the frame has taken up. Everyone learns this by having a cover tear along a seam at three in the morning in a gale, and nobody learns it any other way.

Bronze screws are counted out of the drawer into a tin before the job starts, because counting them back in afterwards is the only way anyone has found to know whether one is still in the hull. The yard has been doing it that way since long before anybody wrote it down, and the one time it was skipped cost a week of pulling planks.

Caulking is driven in one hard pass and one soft one. The hard pass is what holds and the soft pass is what stops the hard pass working its way back out over a season of flexing. Neither is visible once the seam is payed, which is the general condition of every part of this trade that actually matters.

The mooring lines are doubled from November and singled again at Easter, and the yard keeps a list of which boats have been done, because memory at that scale is not reliable and the consequence of being wrong is somebody else's boat on the rocks.

A survey is a snapshot with a signature on it. It reports what the surveyor could reach on the day, which on a boat in the water is perhaps half of what there is, and the report says so in a paragraph nobody reads. The value of the document is almost entirely in that paragraph.

The chandlery closes at four in the winter and there is no second chandlery, so the list is written on Wednesday and bought on Thursday, and anything forgotten costs a week. This is the reason the drawers are labelled and the reason the labels are checked.

Paint over varnish is a repair. Varnish over paint is a decision somebody will regret in front of an audience. The two are told apart by scraping a coin across a hidden corner, which takes four seconds and is skipped roughly half the time.
