On slow tools

A tool that is slow in the right place is often faster than one that is quick everywhere. The question is never how many operations a second the thing can do; it is how many of them you had to ask for.

Consider the difference between a saw that cuts in one direction and one that cuts in both. The second sounds strictly better. In practice the first gives you a rhythm, and rhythm is what carries a long cut straight.

There is a version of this argument that is merely nostalgia, and it should be named before it is dismissed. Old tools were not better because they were old. They were better in the specific cases where the constraint they imposed matched the constraint of the work.

The interesting cases are the ones where removing a constraint made the work worse. A word processor that repaginates continuously removes the page as a unit of thought. A camera that takes a hundred frames a second removes the moment of choosing.

The instrument shapes the question. Change the instrument and you have quietly changed what you were asking.

None of this argues for deliberately crippling anything. It argues for knowing which of your constraints are load-bearing, which is a harder question than it sounds and is almost never answered by benchmarking.

Three tests

  • Does the constraint make a class of mistake impossible, or merely inconvenient? Only the first is worth keeping.
  • Would you notice if it were removed tomorrow, or in six months? The slow-acting ones are the ones that get removed.
  • Can you name the last time it stopped you doing something you actually wanted to do? If not, it may be doing nothing.

The third test is the one people fail. A constraint that never bites is not a safety property; it is a decoration that has been mistaken for one, and it will be removed by the first person who is in a hurry.

A related failure is the constraint that bites constantly. That one does not survive either, because everybody learns to work around it, and a rule everybody routes around is worse than no rule: it teaches people that the rules are advisory.

So the useful constraints sit in a narrow band. They fire often enough that you remember they exist, and rarely enough that firing still means something. That band is where a good tool lives, and it is why so few tools are good.

A worked example

Version control is the clearest case. The commit is a constraint: it makes you stop, name what you did, and draw a boundary around it. Nothing about the machine requires this. You could persist every keystroke and never name anything, and some editors do.

The naming is the whole value. It forces a small act of summary at the exact moment when you still remember why. Six months later that summary is the only thing standing between a reader and an archaeology project.

Notice that the constraint is cheap when the work is coherent and expensive when it is not, which is exactly the right shape. A commit that is hard to name is usually a commit that is doing two things, and being unable to name it is the tool telling you so.

This is the property worth designing for and the hardest one to fake. A warning that fires on everything has no information in it. A warning that fires when something is genuinely wrong is worth interrupting someone for, and it earns the right to interrupt precisely by being rare.

Where this leaves us

Prefer the tool whose refusals you can predict. Prefer the one that tells you what it did rather than what it intended. And when a tool reassures you, check what the reassurance is made of, because the comfortable answer and the true one are not always the same shape.

That last one is the whole discipline, and it is unglamorous. It is mostly a matter of reading the numbers a thing reports and asking which act produced them, which is slower than trusting the summary and is the only method that has ever worked.
