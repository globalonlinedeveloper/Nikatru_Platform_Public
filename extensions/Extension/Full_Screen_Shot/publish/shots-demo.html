A long document, so the part below the fold is the part worth showing

This page exists to be captured. It is taller than the window on purpose: everything past the first screen is what a visible-area screenshot loses and a full-page capture keeps.

Overview

A window shows one screen at a time. A document is rarely one screen long, so a picture of a document is either several pictures stitched together or a picture of its first third. Stitching by hand means scrolling a fixed number of pixels, pressing a key, and hoping the sticky header did not print itself four times down the middle of the result.

The interesting failures are not the long pages. They are the pages with a toolbar that follows the scroll, a panel that scrolls inside the page, a list that only renders the rows you can see, and a frame inside the frame. Each one looks fine on screen and each one comes out wrong if the capture is naive about it.

Notes

  • A sticky element should appear once, where it was, and not once per stitched band.
  • A panel that scrolls inside the page is a second document; it is either unrolled or it is captured as it was seen, and either way the picture should say which.
  • A list that renders only visible rows has no pixels for the rest. Something has to ask it to render them before there is anything to photograph.
  • An image that has not finished decoding photographs as a black rectangle, which is the kind of defect nobody notices until the picture is already in a document.

None of this is visible in a screenshot of the top of the page, which is why the top of the page is a poor place to stop.

Method

Scroll to a position. Wait for the page to settle. Take the visible area. Move down by slightly less than a screen, so the bands overlap and nothing falls between them. Repeat until the bottom stops moving, then align the overlaps and keep one copy of anything that followed the scroll.

The last step is the one that separates a stitched image from a usable one: a toolbar that was pinned to the top of the window belongs at the top of the picture, once.
